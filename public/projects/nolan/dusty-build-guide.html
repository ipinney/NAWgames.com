How To BuildDUSTY

A little robot that sweeps crumbs off the table and stops itself right at the edge. This guide takes you from a pile of parts and a 3D printer to a working robot, one step at a time.

Read this part first

  • A grown-up runs the 3D printer. The nozzle is about 220°C and the bed about 60°C. Never touch either while it is printing or cooling.
  • Hands off while it prints. The printer moves fast and on its own, and this one has no door. Stand back and watch.
  • A grown-up does the soldering. The iron tip is hotter than the printer nozzle. Always put it back in its stand, and wash hands after touching solder.
  • Tiny screws and nuts are choking hazards. Keep them in a cup, and away from little brothers.
  • Only use the cables from the parts list. A plug with the wrong voltage can cook the micro:bit.
  • Switch off and unplug the power bank cable before changing any wire. Every single time.
  • Never crush, pierce or drop the power bank. It is a lithium battery. If it gets hot, swells or smells, a grown-up takes it out and puts it somewhere safe.
  • Gears pinch and brushes grab. Brush switch off before your fingers go near the front.
  • Test on a low table first. A kitchen chair is a shorter drop than a counter.

Meet the parts

Lay everything out before you build anything. Find each item below so you know what you are holding. Every part is also in the 3D model.

micro:bit v2

A tiny computer the size of a credit card cut in half. The 25 red squares are little lights called LEDs. The gold stripes at the bottom are how it plugs into other boards.

Its job: it is Dusty's brain. It reads the sensors, decides what to do, and tells the motors to go.

PLUGS IN FLAT · LEDS FACE UP

moto:bit motor board

A red board with a connector on its front edge that the micro:bit plugs into flat, a round hole for the battery plug, connectors for two motors, a STOP/RUN motor switch, and rows of pins for sensors.

Its job: the middleman. The micro:bit is far too weak to run motors directly, so it whispers instructions to this board, and this board does the heavy lifting.

3 TO 11 VOLTS · DRIVES 2 MOTORS

N20 gear motor ×2

A small silver can with a gold gearbox and a flat-sided metal shaft. Yours have a tiny circuit board on the back and six wires.

Its job: one drives the left wheel, one the right. Only two of the six wires power the motor. The other four are for the encoder, a turn counter Dusty does not use yet.

6 VOLTS · 298:1 GEARBOX · ENCODER

Wheels ×2

Black plastic wheels with a soft rubber tire. The hole in the middle is a circle with one flat side, shaped to match the motor shaft.

Its job: the wheel size decides how fast Dusty drives, and speed is the thing this whole experiment measures. Do not swap for bigger ones.

PRESS-FIT · NO GLUE NEEDED

Ball caster

A little cage holding a 3/8 inch metal ball.

Its job: the third foot at the back. The printed base already has the right height built in, so no spacers are needed.

Two tiny bumps on a small board. One shines invisible infrared light down, the other watches for it to bounce back.

Its job: the whole point of the robot. Light comes back means table. Nothing comes back means the table ended, stop now.

A small black block with a springy metal arm and a little wheel on the end. Push the arm and it clicks.

A plain silver motor with two metal tabs and a thin shaft. It spins thousands of times a minute.

What is PLA?

PLA is a plastic made from plants, usually corn or sugarcane. It comes on a spool as a long thread. The printer pushes the thread into a hot nozzle, about 220°C, where it melts like the glue in a hot glue gun.

The nozzle draws the shape of one layer, a fifth of a millimeter thick. The bed drops a tiny bit, and it draws the next layer on top. The base plate is 28 mm tall, so that is about 140 layers.

PLA goes soft at about 60°C. A car parked in the Houston sun gets hotter than that inside. Never leave Dusty in the car.

What is a brim, and why test first?

A brim is a thin flat ring the printer lays down around each part, like the brim of a hat. It gives the part more grip on the bed so the corners do not curl up. You peel it off afterward.

Batch 1 is a fit check: a few tiny parts that show whether the printer makes holes the right size. Testing the risky thing first, before the long print, is what engineers do.

What does M2 mean?

Screws are named by how thick the thread is. M2 means 2 millimeters across, about as thick as a strand of spaghetti. M2 × 8 means 2 mm thick and 8 mm long.

The holes in the plastic are a little smaller than the screw, 1.8 mm. As the screw turns, its thread cuts a matching groove in the plastic. That is called a self-tapping fit, and the small hole is a pilot hole.

Two kinds of gears in one robot

Inside the gold box on each drive motor is a stack of tiny metal gears. The motor spins very fast with almost no strength. The gears trade speed for strength: a 298:1 ratio means the motor turns 298 times for one turn of the wheel.

You will build a gear set like this yourself in Step 9, for the brush.

Do not over-tighten. If a screw suddenly spins freely, the plastic thread is stripped. Move on and tell a grown-up. A slightly longer screw or a drop of glue fixes it.

You are done when

Both wheels spin freely, both shafts point outward, and the wheels look parallel when you sight down the front.

What even is a microcontroller?

A laptop is a computer that can do millions of different things. A microcontroller is a computer that does one job forever, very reliably, using almost no electricity. There is one in your microwave, one in the car's key fob, and a few dozen in the car itself.

The micro:bit is a microcontroller you can reprogram. When you load code onto it, that code stays there. Unplug it, switch it on a week later, and it starts running your program again immediately. It has no operating system to boot, so it is ready the instant power arrives.

Why can't the micro:bit just run the motors?

Think of the micro:bit's pins as a person who can point but cannot lift. A pin can supply enough electricity to light a small LED, roughly 1/100th of what a motor wants. Wire a motor straight to it and you do not get a slow robot, you get a dead micro:bit.

The driver board contains a circuit called an H-bridge. It is four electronic switches arranged in a shape like the letter H, with the motor in the middle. Close two switches and electricity flows left to right and the motor spins forward. Close the other two and it flows right to left and the motor spins backward. The micro:bit just says which pair to close.

Why a power bank

A power bank holds a steady 5 volts until it is empty. AA batteries start near 6 volts and fade as they run down, which would make Dusty slower as the day goes on. Speed is exactly what the experiment measures, so a steady supply keeps the test fair. It also charges from a phone charger without taking anything apart.

How to charge Dusty

Switch Dusty off. Plug a USB-C phone charger into the port that shows on Dusty’s left side. The bank’s lights are hidden under the deck, so leave it about three hours, or slide it out and press the button to check. Always charge on a hard surface with a grown-up nearby.

What does 40% speed actually mean?

The board switches the power fully on and fully off hundreds of times every second. On 40% of the time, the motor acts like it is getting 40% of the power. This is called PWM, short for pulse width modulation. A dimmer switch works the same way.

At 40%, Dusty drives about 7 centimeters per second. Changing that number is the experiment.

A wheel spins the wrong way? Do not rewire it. Use the invert LEFT motor block (or RIGHT) in on start. Fixed in code, it can never come loose.

You are done when

How does a cliff sensor see?

One bump is a tiny infrared light, the other is a light detector. Infrared is just past red, so your eyes cannot see it, but a phone camera can. Try it.

On the table, the light bounces straight back into the detector. Past the edge, it shines into empty space and almost nothing comes back.

Why 3 mm?

This sensor sees best at about 3 mm and gets unreliable past 6 mm. The slot lets you set the height exactly, and set it again if an arm gets bumped.

What does "analog" mean?

Some questions have two answers: is the button pressed, yes or no. That is digital, and computers love it.

Other questions have a whole range of answers: how much light came back? That is analog. The micro:bit turns it into a number from 0 to 1023, where 0 is none at all and 1023 is loads.

Analog is better here than a simple yes/no, because you get to pick where the line falls. And picking that line on the actual table you are using, instead of guessing once and hoping, is what calibration means.

Why calibrate at all?

A white paper tablecloth might read 200. Dark wood might read 650. If you hard-coded "over 500 means cliff" then Dusty would refuse to move on the dark table, convinced it was already falling.

So instead, Dusty looks at whatever surface it is sitting on, right now, and decides for itself what "normal" looks like. Everything much brighter than normal is a cliff. Same code, any table. That one idea is worth explaining to a judge.

Test on a low table first, and put a cushion on the floor. Your first few runs will have bugs, and a bug here means the robot drives off the edge.

You are done when

Twenty approaches out of twenty end with Dusty stopping and turning away, written down in the notebook. That is the sensor weekend finished.

Add the whisker

Why two of everything?

The infrared sensors can be fooled by a black placemat. The whisker cannot, because it touches instead of looking. Engineers call this redundancy: two systems that fail for different reasons.

When a judge asks “what if the sensor is wrong?”, this is the answer.

You are done when

With the infrared sensors taped over, Dusty still stops at the edge using only the whisker.

Build the brush and the gears

Sat Oct 3 · about 2 hours

Now Dusty earns its name. The brush motor spins far too fast for a brush, so three printed gears slow it down before it reaches the roller.

THE BRUSH GEAR TRAIN, SEEN FROM THE RIGHT SIDE OF THE ROBOT.

How the gears slow the brush down

When two gears touch, their teeth pass each other one for one. The little motor gear has 12 teeth. The big gear has 36. So the motor gear has to go around 3 times to push the big gear around once.

The big gear has a small 10-tooth gear stuck to it, so they always turn together. That small gear drives the 18-tooth roller gear. 18 ÷ 10 is 1.8, so it slows down again.

The two slow-downs multiply:

What do you get for going slower?

Strength. Engineers call turning strength torque. Gearing down by 5.4 times gives the roller about 5.4 times the turning strength, minus a little lost to friction. That is what lets fuzzy bristles push through a pile of cereal without stalling.

A plain 130 motor spins roughly 8,000 to 10,000 times a minute with no load. Divided by 5.4, the roller turns roughly 1,500 to 1,800 times a minute. Still fast, but now it has muscle. This is called gearing down.

Try it: once the gears are in, put a marker dot on one tooth of the motor gear and one on the roller gear. Turn the roller slowly by hand and count how many times the motor gear goes around for one turn of the roller. You should count about five and a half.

How the tray keeps crumbs in

The front of the tray is a ramp that climbs to a little ridge, then drops straight down. Crumbs flicked by the brush fly over the ridge, but once they land behind it they cannot roll back out. It works like a one-way door.

Inside, the corners are sloped instead of square, so crumbs slide to the middle where they are easy to dump out. Along the top of the back and side walls, a small lip leans inward. A crumb that bounces up hits the lip and falls back in.

Why the bottom of the tray slopes

A tray lying flat on the table would rub along its whole bottom, like dragging a book across a desk. That rubbing is called friction, and the motors would waste their push fighting it. So only the thin front edge touches the table, where it scoops crumbs up the ramp. From there the bottom tilts up, and the back of the tray floats 2.5 mm above the table.

The tray does not hold itself up with that front edge. It hangs from the hook at the back of the base and the two side bumps. The front edge only rests on the table, so the rubbing stays tiny. The sides are also cut down in a slope on each side of the snap bumps, which takes off plastic the tray does not need.

Why weigh it?

Saying “Dusty cleans pretty well” is an opinion. Saying “Dusty picked up 3.8 grams out of 5, which is 76 percent” is a measurement. Science runs on measurements.

Keep crumbs off the sensors. If the brush throws crumbs forward onto a sensor, it goes blind. Check the spin direction again, and wipe the sensor faces before each test.

Why 8 rows, 4 cm apart?

The brush is 52 mm wide. If the rows are 40 mm apart, each row overlaps the last one by 12 mm, so no stripe of crumbs gets missed. A foot is about 305 mm, and 305 divided by 40 is about 8 rows.

At 40% Dusty drives about 70 mm every second, so one 305 mm row takes about 4.4 seconds, and a 40 mm slide takes about 0.6 seconds. Those are the starting numbers. The real table decides the final ones.

What should happen?

Faster means more distance to stop. Your prediction: the stopping distance grows faster than the speed. Double the speed and the distance more than doubles, because a faster robot has more to slow down and also covers more ground while the code reacts.

Dusty has a 62 mm head start. The math says that even at 100% it should need less than that, so it should never fall. If your chart shows the distances climbing toward 62, you can draw the line forward and predict the speed where Dusty would fall. That number is the one to put in big type on the board.

For the spill race, a good guess is that Spot Clean wins. Random bounce spends most of its time driving where there are no crumbs. Spot Clean spends all of its time on the spill.

Words to know

Say these like you mean them

Judges ask what words mean. Here they are in one place.

MicrocontrollerA tiny computer that does one job forever, using barely any power.
H-bridgeFour switches that let a motor spin either direction. Inside the moto:bit.
InfraredLight just past red that eyes cannot see. TV remotes use it.
PLAA plastic made from plants. The printer melts it and builds parts in layers.
LayerOne thin slice of a 3D print, 0.2 mm on Dusty. Stack them and you get a part.
Fit checkTesting the part everything else depends on before making the rest.
Pilot holeA hole a bit smaller than the screw, so the screw cuts its own thread.
Gear ratioHow many turns in for one turn out. The brush gears are 5.4 to 1.
Gearing downUsing gears to trade speed for strength.
TorqueTurning strength. What gearing down gives you more of.
PWMFlicking power on and off very fast to make a motor run slower.
AnalogA measurement with a whole range of answers, not just yes or no.
CalibrationLetting the robot measure its own surroundings instead of guessing in advance.
ThresholdThe line you draw in a measurement: above this means cliff, below means table.
Differential driveSteering by running two wheels at different speeds.
RedundancyTwo systems that fail for different reasons, so one catches what the other misses.
