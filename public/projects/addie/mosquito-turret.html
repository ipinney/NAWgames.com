Can a laser kill a mosquito?

Yes, but not a safe one. Scientists measured exactly how much laser energy it takes, and the answer is a great fact for the board.

7 J/cm²
Energy per spot that killed 90% of mosquitoes (blue or green laser, 1/40 of a second flash).
~14 watts
Laser power that takes, for a 2.5 mm spot in that 1/40 of a second.
0.001 watts
Our Class 2 laser.
14,000×
How much weaker ours is than a mosquito-killing flash.

Why we don't use a stronger laser

  • A 14 watt laser is Class 4, the most dangerous class. It can blind an eye instantly, even from a reflection off a shiny surface, and it can start fires.
  • Science fair rules (ISEF) allow only Class 1, 2, 3A, and 3R. Class 3B and Class 4 are not on the list.
  • Going up to the strongest allowed class (5 mW) still leaves us about 2,800 times too weak. It adds risk and does not kill anything.
  • Holding our 1 mW beam on one spot does not add up either: the scientists found longer, weaker flashes kill less, because the heat leaks away.

How the real Photonic Fence stays safe

  • It is built by grown-up laser scientists and works 30 meters away.
  • It will not fire if a person or animal is in the zone.
  • The newer systems use a laser color that is safer for the retina.

Addie's turret does the same finding, tracking, and hit-proving as the real one. The only thing it leaves out is the power, on purpose. That choice goes on the board as part of the science.

Math: 7.1 J/cm² × area of a 2.5 mm spot (0.049 cm²) = 0.35 J. Delivered in 0.025 s = 14 W. 14 W / 0.001 W = 14,000. Source: Keller et al., Scientific Reports, 2020.

The problem

Houston has a lot of mosquitoes. They bite, they itch, and some carry diseases. Scientists have built machines that shoot them out of the air with lasers.

How Design A works

Side view. The mosquito swings in front of a black foam board backdrop. The beam can only end on the backdrop.

  1. Train. Addie points the camera at the mosquito, holds the learn button, and slowly turns the mosquito so the camera learns it from every side and distance. Then she saves it.
  2. Track. The camera reports where the mosquito is on its screen (320 by 240 dots). Too far left, the pan servo turns left. Too high, the tilt servo tips up, but never past the printed stop.
  3. Lock on. When the mosquito is inside a small box in the middle of the screen, the micro:bit shows a target and beeps.
  4. Fire. If the arm switch is on, the laser flashes for a quarter of a second and the speaker plays the firing sound: laser "pew" or cannon "boom", whichever is picked by touching the gold logo on the wand.
  5. Prove it. The laser dot lands on the white pod sewn to the mosquito, and the whole pod glows. The light sensor inside jumps. The wand micro:bit flashes the mosquito's red eyes, plays a splat, shows an X, and radios "HIT". The turret adds a point and plays a victory sound.

Experiments and data

The build answers the engineering goal. The experiment answers the science question. Two tests, 30 runs, all recorded by the machine itself, done over winter break.

Engineering goal: Can I build a robot that finds, tracks, and hits a flying mosquito by itself?
Science question: What makes the MS-2000 miss more: a faster mosquito or a smaller one?

Hits are how accuracy is measured. Each test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same, so the test that drops the hits the most is the biggest reason the MS-2000 misses.

Hypothesis

If the mosquito is   (faster or smaller), then the MS-2000 will get   hits, because  . One for each test.

I think   will make it miss the most, because  .

Variables

  • Change (one per test): swing angle (farther back means faster) or the size of the target hole
  • Measure: hits in 30 seconds, and time to the first hit
  • Keep the same: same turret, mosquito, line length, room lights, camera training, and fresh batteries. Every run is 3 feet from the camera, the distance the laser is lined up for. When one thing changes, the other stays at 10° or the 40 mm hole.

How one run works

  1. On the wand, pick the test and setting with button A, and the run number with button B.
  2. Hang the wand on the hook, pull the mosquito to the angle, let go.
  3. Press start. The MS-2000 hunts for 30 seconds by itself.
  4. The wand beeps at the end and saves one row of data.
  5. Write the hits on the paper data sheet too, as a backup.

What gets saved for every run

  • Test (speed or size)
  • Setting (for example 20°)
  • Run number (1 to 5)
  • Hits in 30 seconds
  • Time to the first hit
  • Room light level, to check the room stayed the same

The micro:bit V2 keeps this data even when the batteries come out. Plugged into a computer, the file called MY_DATA opens as a table with graphs and can be downloaded for a spreadsheet.

Data rules

  • Every run counts. Bad runs stay in the data.
  • Five runs for every setting, never fewer.
  • If something breaks, write down what happened and redo that run.
  • Do each test on one day so the room stays the same.
  • Take a photo of every setup.

Turning data into answers

  • Average: add the 5 runs and divide by 5.
  • One bar graph per test, averages on the bars.
  • Compare: which setting got the most hits, and by how much?
  • Answer the question: which test dropped the hits the most? That is the biggest reason the MS-2000 misses.
  • Conclusion: "My hypothesis was (right / wrong) because the data showed ___."
  • What I would change next time.

Laser safety

These are the rules the big international fair (ISEF) uses, which many school fairs copy. Our school may be stricter, so we ask the teacher before ordering.

What ISEF says

  • Class 1, 2, 3A, and 3R lasers are allowed "to be used responsibly".
  • Lasers must carry the maker's label showing their power.
  • Handheld lasers are not allowed.
  • No beams through magnifiers or telescopes.
  • Moving parts with pinch points must be covered.
  • Unsafe lasers get taken away with no warning.

What we do

  • Class 2, the lowest visible class, under 1 mW, labeled
  • Bolted into the printed head, never held
  • Printed tilt stop plus code limits: it cannot aim past the backdrop
  • Black foam backdrop behind the mosquito stops every shot; no mirrors or shiny parts near the beam
  • Short flashes only when locked on, only with the arm switch on
  • The wand holder stands beside the turret, never between the turret and the backdrop
  • Servo gears covered

Where the facts came from

  • Keller et al., Optical tracking and laser-induced mortality of insects during flight, Scientific Reports (2020): lethal doses of about 7 J/cm² at 445 and 532 nm with a 2.5 mm beam and 25 ms pulse; longer pulses killed less.
  • An optical system to detect, surveil, and kill flying insect vectors, Scientific Reports (2024): works at 30 m; will not fire when people or animals enter the zone.
  • ScienceAlert, Photonic Fence: finds bugs by wingbeat, size and flight; fires in under 100 milliseconds.
  • Society for Science, ISEF Display and Safety Rules: laser classes, labels, no handheld lasers, pinch points.
  • Quarton VLM-650-03 manual: the LPT version is under 1 mW, Class 2, with a safety label.
  • DFRobot: HuskyLens object tracking with micro:bit: hold the learn button to learn an object at different angles and distances; the micro:bit gets its X and Y position.
  • micro:bit, Data logging user guide: the micro:bit V2 saves data that survives power off and opens as MY_DATA with tables, graphs, and CSV download.
  • American Mosquito Control Association, FAQs: flight speed, only females bite, CO2 from 35 m, 174 US species, 2,700 worldwide.
