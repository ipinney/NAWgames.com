How To BuildMS-2000

A laser turret that learns what a mosquito looks like, follows it, and tags it with a safe laser. The mosquito flashes its eyes on every hit, and the wand saves every run as data. This guide goes from a box of parts to a working turret, one step at a time.

Read this part first

  • Never look into the laser. Not even a weak one. Point it only at the backdrop and the mosquito.
  • The arm switch stays off until a grown-up turns it on. No arm switch, no laser, no matter what the code says.
  • The beam runs sideways along the table, never toward people. The printed tilt stop keeps the head from aiming up.
  • Only the Class 2 laser on the parts list. Its maker label stays showing. Never swap in a laser pointer.
  • Soldering is a grown-up job. The iron is hotter than an oven. Wear eye protection.
  • The laser's metal case carries power. Only plastic touches it. No metal clips or screws against the case.
  • Fingers away from the servos while the turret is on. The clutch servos slip instead of pinching hard, but they still move fast.
  • Side switch off and bank cable unplugged before any wiring change. Side switch off when you are done for the day.
  • The power bank is a sealed lithium battery. Never crush, pierce, or drop it. If it gets hot, swells, or smells, a grown-up takes it out and puts it somewhere safe.

The big picture

How the MS-2000 works

Two micro:bits work as a team. The one in the turret sees, aims, and fires. The one in the wand feels the hit and keeps score. They talk by radio.

All seven steps happen in less than a second.

Why two micro:bits?

The turret can not see the laser dot land. It only knows where it aimed. To prove a real hit, something has to be inside the target and feel the light. That is the wand's job. Having one machine shoot and a different machine check the score is how real tests stay honest.

Know your gear

Meet the parts

Lay everything out on a towel before you build. Find each item below so you know what you are holding. Every bought part is also in 3D, with its size.

micro:bit V2 ×2

A tiny computer with 25 lights, two buttons, a touch logo, a speaker, a microphone, a light sensor, and a radio built in. The five big gold rings are where wires connect.

Its job: one is the turret's brain. The other lives in the wand, feels hits, and saves the data.

51.6 × 42 mm · must be V2 (has the gold logo)

Xia mi board

A helper board the micro:bit plugs into. It adds 5 volt pins for servos, a special plug for the camera, a relay, and a jack for the power cable.

Its job: the turret's power station and switchboard. Its relay is the switch that fires the laser.

57 × 87 mm · power switch stays ON

HuskyLens AI camera

A camera with a tiny AI chip. Show it an object, hold the learn button, and it remembers it. Its screen draws a box around the object and it tells the micro:bit where the box is.

Its job: the turret's eyes. It finds the mosquito 30 times a second or so.

52 × 36 mm · 2 × M3 screws · own cable included

Clutch servo ×3

A small motor that turns to an exact angle, from 0 to 180 degrees, and holds it. The white arm is the horn. A clutch inside slips if a hand grabs it, so the gears do not break.

Its job: one turns the head left and right (pan), one tips it up and down (tilt). The third is a spare.

23 × 12 mm body · 3-pin plug

Class 2 laser

A red laser, less than one thousandth of a watt, in a metal tube as thin as a pencil. The label says Class 2: safe enough that your blink protects you, but still never look into it.

Its job: it rides under the camera and flashes for a quarter of a second when the mosquito is centered.

7 mm × 21 mm · 650 nm red · plastic holder only

Arm switch

A slide switch smaller than a fingernail. The middle leg connects to one side or the other, depending on where the lever sits.

Its job: the laser safety. It sits in the back wall. When it is off, the laser has no power at all.

About 9 × 4 mm · hot glued in its frame

Light sensor ×3

A tiny sensor that lets more electricity through when more light hits it. The micro:bit reads it as a number from 0 (dark) to 1023 (very bright).

Its job: it hides inside the mosquito pod and feels the laser dot. Two are spares.

7.8 × 10.6 mm · VIN, GND, OUT

Red LEDs and resistors ×10 each

An LED is a light that only works one way round: long leg to plus. A resistor slows electricity down so the LED does not burn out.

Their job: two LEDs are the mosquito's eyes. Each gets its own 220 ohm resistor.

5 mm LEDs · push in from the back of the pod

Speaker module

A speaker with its own amplifier, which makes quiet signals loud, and a volume knob.

Its job: the pew, the boom, the lock-on beep, and the victory song. It faces the grille in the back of the base.

40 × 40 mm · 4 × M2 · Gravity cable

Power bank and battery holders

The power bank is a rechargeable battery, like the one that charges a phone. It has a USB-A port that gives power and a USB-C port that charges it. The small holders take two AAA batteries and have the white plug a micro:bit takes.

Their job: the bank powers the turret and stays inside the base. You charge it through a USB-C port on the side. A small pack powers the wand.

Bank 97 × 46 × 22 mm, 5 V · 2×AAA zip-tied

Four thin wires stuck side by side, soft and bendy. It rides down the fishing line to the mosquito, held on by the little printed clips.

What is a circuit?

Electricity only flows around a complete loop: out of the battery, through the part, and back. Every part here has two paths: power in and ground back. Break the loop anywhere and nothing happens. That is exactly how the arm switch works: it breaks the laser's loop.

Part A

1

Unbox and measure

The weekend the parts arrive · about 1 hour

The print files were drawn from datasheets and photos before anything arrived. A part that is 1 mm different can stop a screw from lining up. Ten minutes with calipers now saves a 3 hour reprint.

  1. Check every item against the shopping list. Tick them off on the parts list PDF.Keep the tiny parts (sensors, LEDs, resistors, switch) in a labeled bag.
  2. Measure the Xia mi board: the distance between its corner holes, both ways.The base expects about 80.2 mm by 50.2 mm between hole centers.
  3. Measure a servo, the 2×AAA pack, the arm switch, the power bank, the in-line switch, the charge port, and the stuffed mosquito. Write every number in the journal.The bank, the in-line switch, and the charge port all fit holes or a cradle in the base, so measure them before the base prints.
  4. Charge the power bank. Plug a USB-C phone charger into its USB-C port. It takes about 2 to 2.5 hours.
  5. Read the Xia mi board for a version number (V1 or V2). You need it for the relay block in Step 11.
  6. Plug a micro:bit into the Xia mi. Plug the 90° adapter and the barrel cable into the bank's USB-A port, the cable into the in-line switch, and the switch into the DC jack. Switch on.   7. grown-up Voltage check: with both servos sweeping and the laser on, the Xia mi 5V pins must read at least 4.6 V on a meter.The bank gives 5 V, the lowest the DC jack takes. If it reads low, plug into the board's own USB 5 V input instead, with a USB cable that has an in-line switch.
  8. A grown-up compares your numbers with the print files and fixes anything that is off before printing.

What are calipers, and what is tolerance?

Calipers are a ruler with jaws. Close them on a part and read the size to a tenth of a millimeter.

No two parts are exactly the same size. The small wiggle room between "the size it should be" and "the size it really is" is called tolerance. Printed holes are drawn 0.3 mm bigger on each side so parts slide in even when things are a little off.

Why is the inside of a print not solid?

Solid plastic would take hours longer and waste filament. So the printer draws a strong outer skin (the walls) and fills the inside with a wavy pattern called gyroid infill, only 15% full. It works like the honeycomb inside a beehive: light, but strong in every direction.

The base is heavier because it is so big, and that is good. A heavy base does not tip when the head swings fast.

PLA goes soft at about 60°C. A car in the Houston sun gets hotter than that. Never leave the MS-2000 in the car.

Part B

The mosquito and the wand

Build the target first. Once a flashlight can make the mosquito's eyes flash, you know the hardest part of the proof works, before the turret even exists.

3

Wire the mosquito pod

Batches 1 and 2 done · about 1.5 hours · grown-up solders

The pod is the mosquito's hidden heart: a white cup that glows when the laser hits, a light sensor inside, and two red eyes on top.

The dot can land anywhere on the 40 mm face and still count.

  1. Pick a job for each ribbon wire and write it in the journal: 3V power, ground, sensor signal, eyes.Red for 3V and black for ground are good habits.
  2. Push the two red LEDs into the eye holes from the back of the eye bar. Long legs on the same side.
  3. Solder grown-up the light sensor: 3V wire to VIN, ground wire to GND, signal wire to OUT.
  4. Solder the eyes: each long leg to its own 220 ohm resistor, both resistor ends to the eyes wire, both short legs to the ground wire.Tape or a dab of hot glue over each joint so no bare metal can touch.
  5. Seat the sensor in the cap's pocket, face out, with the wires and resistors in the open back.
  6. Glue the cap into the white cup. Thread the ribbon through the entry on the eye bar.
  7. Tie the fishing line through the tab on top. Double knot.
  8. Sew the pod onto the mosquito's back through the four lug holes. Wait to do this until Step 5 works.

Why a white cup instead of just the sensor?

The light sensor is smaller than a fingernail. The laser dot is about the size of a pea, and it does not always land dead center. At 2 feet and 4 feet it lands about 13 mm off from where the camera aims.

So the sensor hides behind a light collector. White plastic scatters light, so a dot anywhere on the face makes the whole cup glow, and the sensor sees the glow. The eyes sit outside the cup so their own light never fools the sensor.

You are done when

The pod is closed up, four labeled wires come out of it, and no bare metal shows.

4

Build the wand

Same weekend · about 45 minutes

The wand is a handle for the second micro:bit and its batteries. It clips onto the rod for the hand-held demo, and sits by the pendulum pivot for experiments. No soldering here: screws clamp the wires.

Purple wand at the top, ribbon riding the line down to the pod.

  1. Lay the wand micro:bit face up in its bay, gold rings toward the line end. Slide the top edge under the two corner lips.
  2. Strip about 8 mm off the end of each ribbon wire and twist the strands tight.
  3. Clamp each wire: wrap it clockwise around an M2 screw, then drive the screw down through its ring into the boss. 3V wire to 3V, ground to GND, signal to P0, eyes to P1.Clockwise, so tightening the screw pulls the wire in instead of pushing it out.
  4. Put the 2×AAA pack in its bay and push its plug into the white socket on the micro:bit.
  5. Zip-tie the wand to the rod: one tie at the front, two over the battery pack. The V-shaped rails center the rod.
  6. Slide a line clip onto the fishing line every 10 cm and snap the ribbon into each one. Rod clips hold it along the rod.
Stray strands are trouble. If one wire whisker touches the next ring, 3V and ground can short and the batteries get hot. Trim anything sticking out.

5

Code the wand: prove a hit

The next weekend · about 1.5 hours

The first real test of the whole project. By the end, a flashlight flick on the pod makes the eyes flash, plays a splat, and sends "HIT" by radio. A laser dot will do the same thing later.

A hit is the number jumping above the line. It has to drop back below before the next hit can count.

  1. Go to makecode.microbit.org and start a new project called wand.
  2. Load Program 1 and watch the number scroll. Write down the number in room light.
  3. Shine the flashlight on the white face from about 1 meter. Write down that number too.The two numbers should be far apart. If they are close, check the sensor wires.
  4. Load Program 2. It measures the room when it starts and sets its own threshold.
  5. Flick the flashlight across the pod 20 times. Count the flashes.
  6. If it misses flicks, make the + 100 smaller. If it counts hits with no flashlight, make it bigger.

What is a threshold, and why does the wand pick its own?

A threshold is a line you draw in a measurement: above it means hit, below it means no hit.

Room light is different on a sunny afternoon than at night, and the tests happen on different days. If the line were stuck at one number, the wand could count hits in a bright room that never happened, or miss real ones in a dim one. So the wand measures the room when it starts and puts the line just above that. Measuring your surroundings instead of guessing is called calibration. Judges love that word.

Try it: cover the pod with your hand and press the reset button on the back. Now uncover it. Does it count a hit by itself? Why?

You are done when

Twenty flashlight flicks give twenty flashes, with no extra hits when the flashlight is off. Now sew the pod onto the mosquito.

Journal: the room number, the flashlight number, your threshold gap, and how many of the 20 flicks counted.

Center the servos first

15 minutes · before any horn goes on

A servo can only turn 180 degrees. If the horn goes on while the servo is at one end, the head can only turn one way. So both servos go to the middle, 90 degrees, before anything is attached to them.

  1. Plug the pan servo into P1 and the tilt servo into P2, brown wire toward G.
  2. Plug the charged power bank into the DC jack (adapter, barrel cable, and in-line switch, like in Step 1) and load the centering program.
  3. Turn the in-line switch on. Both servos twitch to the middle and stay there.

How does a servo know what angle to go to?

The micro:bit sends the servo a short pulse about 50 times a second. The length of each pulse is the message: a short pulse means one end, a long pulse means the other end, and in between means in between. A tiny sensor inside the servo checks where the shaft is and keeps turning until it matches. That checking is a feedback loop.

Pan and tilt, and why the head has a stop

Pan is turning left and right, like shaking your head no. Tilt is tipping up and down, like nodding yes. With both, the head can point anywhere in front of it. Security cameras and movie cameras use the same idea.

A printed finger on the left side hits a block if the head tips more than about 40 degrees up or 35 down. Even if the code goes wrong, the laser physically cannot point up at a face. A safety that does not depend on code is called a hard stop.

Do not force the head past its stops by hand. The clutch will slip and click, which is fine once, but it is not a toy.

What is a relay?

A relay is a switch flipped by a tiny electromagnet instead of a finger. The micro:bit sends a small signal, the magnet pulls a metal arm, and a separate circuit closes. You can hear it: click. That click is the moment the laser fires.

NO means normally open: no connection until the relay clicks. COM is the common terminal that gets connected.

Point the turret at the backdrop before a grown-up slides the arm switch on for the first time.
How to charge: side switch OFF, then plug a USB-C phone charger into the side port. About 2 to 2.5 hours. The bank's four lights are hidden inside, so charge before every session. Never charge while it runs: plugging in can blink the power and restart the micro:bit.

Part D

Teach it and code it

The turret learns the mosquito, follows it, and fires when it is centered. Then the laser gets lined up.

10

Teach the camera the mosquito

About 30 minutes

This is machine learning, done with one button. The camera studies the mosquito from lots of angles and builds its own idea of what a mosquito looks like.

  1. Hang the mosquito in front of the black backdrop and switch the turret on.
  2. Check the camera's settings: in General Settings, Protocol Type should be I2C or Auto.
  3. Turn the dial on top until the screen says Object Tracking.
  4. Point the camera so the mosquito sits inside the frame in the middle of the screen.
  5. Press and hold the learn button. While holding it, slowly move the mosquito closer, farther, and turn it a little. Let go.
  6. A box labeled ID1 now follows the mosquito. Swing it around and watch.

What is machine learning?

Normal code is a list of rules someone writes. Nobody can write rules for "what a mosquito looks like." So instead, the camera looks at many examples and finds the pattern itself. That is machine learning.

A camera picture is made of tiny dots called pixels. The camera tells the micro:bit where the middle of the box is, as two numbers: x (across) and y (down). Those are coordinates.

Try it: hold up a different stuffed animal. Does the camera get fooled? Teach it again from more sides and try once more. Write down what happened.
Teach it where it will work: same backdrop, same lights. If the camera forgets after being turned off, save the model in its settings menu, or just teach it again before each session. It takes 30 seconds.

You are done when

The ID1 box stays on the mosquito while it swings, and does not jump to other things in the room.

Why divide by 20?

If the mosquito is 40 pixels to the right, 40 ÷ 20 = 2 degrees of turn. If it is 100 pixels away, the turn is 5 degrees. Big miss, big turn. Small miss, small turn. This is called proportional control, and it is how cruise control, thermostats, and drones steady themselves.

Divide by a small number and the turret over-shoots and wobbles. Divide by a big number and it is smooth but slow. Finding the best number is tuning. Write down each number you try and what happened.

Why wait a whole second between shots?

The camera sends a new picture many times a second. Without a wait, the turret would fire again and again at one lock-on, and one real hit would look like five. The cooldown makes every shot a separate try, so the data is fair. It also gives the eyes time to flash and the splat time to play.

What is sound, and how does the editor make a pew?

Sound is air shaking. How many times it shakes each second is its frequency, measured in hertz. A high shake is a high pitch. A pew is a high pitch that slides down fast. A boom is a very low, rough shake called noise.

You are done when

With the arm switch off, every lock-on beeps, clicks, and pews, no more than once a second. A flashlight flick on the pod makes the turret play Power Up.

13

Line up the laser

About 45 minutes · grown-up runs the arm switch

The laser sits 38 mm below the camera lens, aimed up a tiny bit (about 2.4 degrees) so the two lines cross 3 feet away, the test distance. Now check that the dot really lands on the white face.

Not to scale: the real angle is much flatter. From 2 to 4 feet the dot stays on the face.

  1. Hang the mosquito so the pod is at camera height, 3 feet from the lens, backdrop behind it.
  2. Wand on, turret on, hunting on (testing setup from Step 12).
  3. grown-up Arm switch on. Hold the mosquito still in the middle of the camera's view.
  4. Watch where the dot lands on each shot. Every shot should make the eyes flash.
  5. If the dot is off to one side, arm switch off, loosen the clamp screw, slide a thin strip of paper along one side of the laser to nudge it, tighten, and try again.
  6. Repeat at 2 feet (the fair table) and 4 feet. Arm switch off when you are done.

Why does the dot move with distance?

Two straight lines that are not side by side can only cross at one point. The camera and laser are 38 mm apart, so they cross at 3 feet and drift apart before and after. The white face is 40 mm tall, big enough to catch the dot from 2 to 4 feet. That is why every test runs at 3 feet, and why the fair table keeps the mosquito 2 to 3 feet from the camera.

You are done when

Ten shots out of ten make the eyes flash at 2, 3, and 4 feet, with the mosquito held still.

Journal: where the dot landed at each distance and anything you had to fix.

14

Set up the backdrop and pendulum

Batch 5 done · about 45 minutes

For experiments, nobody holds the mosquito. It hangs from a pivot and swings from a marked angle, so every run starts exactly the same way. That is what makes the test fair.

Pull the mosquito to the angle mark, let go, and it swings side to side in front of the turret.

  1. Stand the foam board in the two printed feet, one near each end.
  2. Push the clip onto the middle of the top edge, and slide the dowel through it so it sticks straight out toward the turret.
  3. Clamp the pendulum pivot near the end of the dowel, angle marks facing the turret.
  4. Loop the fishing line over the peg and adjust it until the white pod hangs at camera height.
  5. Set the wand beside the pivot on the dowel, with the ribbon running down the line.
  6. Tape a tape measure to the table from the line toward the turret. Mark 3 feet (every test run) and 2 feet (the fair table). Put a strip of tape where the turret's front edge goes for each.
  7. Learn the marks: one hole next to a notch is 10 degrees, two holes 20, three holes 30.
  8. Cut three target covers from black paper, each just bigger than the pod face, with a round hole in the middle: 40 mm, 20 mm, and 10 mm. Tape the 40 mm one on for the speed test.Same outside size on all three, so the camera sees the same mosquito and only the target changes.

Why does a bigger angle make a faster mosquito?

Pulling the mosquito back to a higher angle lifts it higher, which gives it more energy. At the bottom of the swing, that energy turns into speed. About twice the angle gives about twice the speed.

Here is the surprise: for small swings, the time for one swing hardly changes. Only the speed does. Galileo noticed this more than 400 years ago watching a lamp swing in a church.

Try it: count the swings in 10 seconds from 10 degrees, then from 30 degrees. Write both numbers down. Are they close?
Beam direction: set the table up so the laser points at the wall of the room, not at a doorway or a couch where people sit.

What is data logging?

Logging means writing down a measurement every time something happens, automatically. The micro:bit V2 saves each row in its own memory, which keeps it even with the batteries out.

Plug the wand into a computer and open the file called MY_DATA on the MICROBIT drive. It shows a table, a graph, and a button to download the numbers for a spreadsheet.

Never add a "delete log" block. Download MY_DATA after every test day and keep a copy. Also write each run on the paper sheet, as a backup.

The first full test

About 1 hour · then practice until Dec 11

A dress rehearsal. Five practice runs, just like a real experiment, to find anything silly before the numbers count.

  1. Charged power bank in the turret, fresh batteries in the wand. Turret on the 5 foot mark, room lights on.
  2. Teach the camera the mosquito if it has forgotten.
  3. On the wand: A until it says speed 10, B to run 1.
  4. grown-up Arm switch on. Pull the mosquito to the 10 degree mark.
  5. Let go, then press A+B. Hands off. Wait for the beep.
  6. Write the hits on the paper data sheet. Press B for the next run. Do five.
  7. Arm switch off. Plug in the wand, open MY_DATA, and check the five rows match the paper.
  8. Take a photo of the setup for the board.

Why practice runs do not count

Practice is for finding problems: a loose wire, a camera that forgot, a threshold that is too tight. Fix them now. Once the real tests start on December 12, every run counts, even the bad ones, and you only redo a run if something broke.

Words to know

Say these like a scientist

Judges ask what words mean. Here they are in one place.

MicrocontrollerA tiny computer that runs one program over and over. The micro:bit is one.
Machine learningA computer finding a pattern from examples instead of written rules.
PixelOne tiny dot in a camera picture. The HuskyLens sees 320 across and 240 down.
CoordinatesTwo numbers that say where something is: x across, y down.
ServoA motor that turns to an exact angle and holds it.
Pan and tiltTurning left and right, and tipping up and down.
Feedback loopLook, adjust, look again, over and over.
Proportional controlA bigger miss gets a bigger correction.
TuningTrying numbers until a machine behaves just right.
Hard stopA physical block that works even if the code is wrong.
RelayA switch flipped by an electromagnet. The click fires the laser.
CircuitA complete loop that electricity flows around.
ResistorA part that slows electricity down to protect an LED.
AnalogA measurement with a whole range of answers, like 0 to 1023.
ThresholdThe line in a measurement between yes and no.
CalibrationMeasuring your surroundings before you start, instead of guessing.
Light collectorThe white cup that turns a dot anywhere on its face into a glow.
CooldownA wait after each shot so one hit is never counted twice.
FrequencyHow many times a sound shakes each second, in hertz.
Radio groupA channel number. Only micro:bits on the same group hear each other.
Data loggingA machine writing down its own measurements.
ToleranceHow far a real part can be from its drawing and still fit.
InfillThe honeycomb pattern inside a 3D print.
Class 2 laserA visible laser under 1 milliwatt. Your blink protects you, but never stare.
